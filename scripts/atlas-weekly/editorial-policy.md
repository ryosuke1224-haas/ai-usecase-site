# Atlas editorial policy

## Target audience

Atlas primarily serves employees and business professionals who want to use AI in their daily work, managers and business leaders who want to improve how their organizations operate, and small and medium-sized business owners who want to adopt AI with limited resources.

## Reader value

Help readers understand what AI developments mean for them and what opportunities, capabilities, or decisions those developments create. Use news as a starting point for investigation. Let the strongest finding determine the subject of the article. A major enterprise AI partnership, for example, could prompt an investigation into whether smaller businesses can access similar capabilities, what they cost, and how they could put them to work.

Go beyond attaching generic business advice to an announcement. Familiar observations about measuring ROI, checking AI outputs, starting small, or keeping people in the loop earn their place when backed by specific, non-obvious insights, evidence, methods, examples, or practical guidance.

Leave readers with something substantive. That could be a capability they did not know existed, a technique they can adopt, a clearer view of costs or trade-offs, a business opportunity, a solution to a workplace problem, or a new way to approach an important decision. An article can be actionable by changing how someone thinks or decides. Use a tutorial or suggest next steps when the topic calls for them.

## Emerging tools and capabilities

Actively cover emerging AI tools, products, technologies, and capabilities as subjects in their own right. Show what someone can now do that was previously difficult, expensive, or out of reach. A focused product article, tutorial or workflow, comparison with an existing approach, business implication, or broader technology shift can each stand alone.

Explore the implications that matter for the story, including productivity, operating models, economics, new products or services, and opportunities for employees, managers, entrepreneurs, and small businesses. Explain relevant skills, costs, and accessibility. Verify current availability, pricing, and technical claims when they matter to the reader's decision. Label hypothetical business opportunities and future possibilities and explain what they depend on. Make room for curiosity and discovery alongside reliability.

## Use AI. Build with AI. Transform with AI.

**Use AI.** Help readers adopt AI and improve everyday work. Cover fundamentals for people getting started and techniques for readers already using AI in analysis, writing, coding, and operations.

**Build with AI.** Help readers build reusable work environments, tools, knowledge bases, data infrastructure, and automated workflows. Explore how persistent business context and reliable data make AI more useful, how teams can reuse validated analyses and business definitions, and what recurring requests reveal about opportunities to automate.

Show what readers can build today that used to be difficult, expensive, or out of reach. When it serves the story, name the technologies and tools that make it possible, explain how to get started, and spell out the skills, data, and resources needed. Show how a one-time AI task can become a reusable workflow, tool, or working environment, and how individuals and small businesses can adopt capabilities once limited to large enterprises.

**Transform with AI.** Explore how AI can reshape business operations, decision-making, organizational productivity, and business opportunities. Consider how teams can move from answering requests to surfacing emerging issues, test decisions with simulations, redesign operating models, and turn productivity gains into lasting value.

Consider enterprise teams and small businesses with limited resources. Show where a simple, affordable implementation can make a sophisticated capability accessible. Help readers judge what fits their current situation, including when a modest change is enough and what would justify a more advanced system.

The aim is to help people use AI and build better ways of working with it. These are editorial interests. Writers choose which ideas serve the story and how to present them.

## Editorial judgment

Consider competing angles before assigning the story. Choose the evidence-backed thesis with the strongest combination of reader value, novelty, curiosity, and practical or strategic opportunity. Ask what would change for the reader and whether the current focus is the opportunity itself or a technique that enables it. A precisely scoped technical or governance article can be the most valuable angle when the problem and payoff warrant that focus. Treat possible future capabilities as possibilities and explain what they depend on.

Balance reliability and risk awareness with curiosity, creativity, and the excitement of building something useful. Give readers a concrete sense of what they could make or try. Let governance, validation, and human review take the lead when they are central to the story. Elsewhere, give them the space the evidence and stakes warrant.

Value analysis people can trust and reproduce. When exploring a change in analytical conditions, first reproduce and reconcile a known-good baseline. Look for ways to reuse earlier analyses, SQL, presentations, workflows, and organizational knowledge. Give particular attention to systems that create value repeatedly and to lessons grounded in real implementation experience.

In technical troubleshooting stories, include the actual error message, reproducible commands, before-and-after examples, and the verified fix when those details are available and safe to disclose. Explain what the verification established and omit sensitive details.

Choose topics, pursue relevant research, and develop original angles. News is one possible starting point. A workplace problem, a useful technique, or a real implementation can be equally good starting points. Skip a news item if it cannot lead to a meaningful insight for Atlas readers. Ground the analysis in evidence and be clear about limitations and uncertainty.

Choose the length, headings, and narrative flow that best serve the story. Treat this policy as editorial guidance, with freedom over structure and wording. Factual accuracy, source verification, confidentiality, original writing, and human approval remain required.
